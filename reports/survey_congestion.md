# Literature survey: Predicting congestion on mining haul roads — is BPR the right tool?

Prepared 2026-08-26 for the WBN nickel haulage simulator (68 km single-lane-per-direction corridor, 30t trucks, multi-contractor traffic, BPR-priced congestion with headway-based capacity).

All sources were located and read (abstract/snippet level or full text) online on 2026-08-26. Citation format: author, year, venue, URL. Where a stable DOI page was paywalled/403, the Google Scholar cluster URL is given (resolves to all versions incl. open PDFs).

---

## 1. Traffic flow / congestion work specific to mine haul roads

**Finding: the mining literature almost never uses volume-delay functions. It models congestion mechanistically — discrete-event simulation (DES) with explicit truck objects, bunching/no-overtaking logic, queueing theory, and (recently) intersection microsimulation.**

- **Zeng, Baafi & Walker, 2019, *International Journal of Mining, Reclamation and Environment* 33(2), "A simulation model to study bunching effect of a truck-shovel system."** https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17480930.2017.1348284
  The key mine-specific congestion paper. They argue explicitly that to capture interactions between hauling trucks "the microscopic simulation approach should be applied by developing the model at the single-vehicle level." Their DES (TSJSim) models each truck, enforces no-overtaking on haul segments so faster trucks are held behind slower ones, and shows lost production is the **combined** result of truck bunching on roads and queuing at shovels/dumps — i.e., macroscopic models that only count queue time at endpoints understate losses. Bunching loss grows with fleet heterogeneity (mixed truck types/payload variance).
- **Zeng, W., 2018, PhD thesis, University of Wollongong, "A simulation model for truck-shovel operation" (TSJSim).** https://ro.uow.edu.au/ndownloader/files/50389686/1
  Full development of the above. Explicitly classifies prior truck-haulage models as "macroscopic" and faults them for ignoring "truck bunching or traffic congestion on the haul routes"; compares truck-allocation strategies with bunching represented.
- **Krzyzanowska, J., 2007, *Journal of the SAIMM* 107, "The impact of mixed fleet hauling on mining operations at Venetia mine."** https://scholar.google.com/scholar?cluster=1891545109380284450 (open PDF via journals.co.za)
  Field study of a shared road used by a mixed fleet (directly analogous to WBN's multiple contractors): documents speed differentials and the bunching that follows when slower units cannot be passed, and its production impact. Cited ~61 times as the canonical field evidence for bunching on mine roads.
- **Soofastaei, Aminossadati, Kizil et al., 2016, *International Journal of Mining Science and Technology* 26, "A comprehensive investigation of loading variance influence on fuel consumption and gas emissions in mine haulage operation."** https://scholar.google.com/scholar?cluster=7999743712021883518
  Payload variance → speed variance → **truck bunching** → fleet productivity and fuel penalties in large surface mines. Bunching modeled as slower loaded trucks pacing the platoon; also his 2016 UQ thesis (https://espace.library.uq.edu.au, "Development of an advanced data analytics model...") links rolling resistance, payload, speed and bunching in an ANN model.
- **Yildirim, M., 2021, PhD dissertation (ProQuest), "Haul Truck Speed Analysis and Effect on Fleet Optimization."** https://scholar.google.com/scholar?cluster=13808664743693246821
  Builds a haul-road "traffic congestion status" calculator and a new **intersection passing algorithm** for mine haul road junction points — one of the few works treating mine intersections (a gap you flagged) explicitly.
- **Miao & Zhao, 2024, *Applied Sciences* (MDPI), "Traffic congestion scheduling for underground mine ramps based on an improved genetic scheduling algorithm."** https://scholar.google.com/scholar?cluster=10214262181431050954
  Single-lane ramp with passing bays: congestion handled by *scheduling meets at passing bays*, not by a delay function. Same structural problem as a single-lane corridor: capacity is set by conflict resolution, not by free-flow headways.
- **Gamache, Basilico, Frayret et al., 2023, *International Journal of Mining, Reclamation and Environment*, "Real-time multi-agent fleet management strategy for autonomous underground mines vehicles."** https://scholar.google.com/scholar?cluster=2364442530259001543
  Multi-agent conflict resolution routing trucks to nearest passing bay; congestion emerges from interactions, again no VDF.
- **Goli, Moniri-Morad, Baart, Shishvan et al., 2025, Proc. (rpsonline), "Improving Safety in Hauling Operations: Predicting and Analyzing Collision Probabilities with Discrete Event Simulation."** https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Improving+Safety+in+Hauling+Operations%22+Goli
  DES modeling dynamic vehicle interactions across **all road segments** (not just load/dump nodes) for risk; same architecture serves congestion.
- **Luan et al., 2026, *Journal of Computational Design and Engineering* (OUP), "Intelligent scheduling control for coordinated passing of multiple autonomous trucks at intersections in open-pit coal mines via enhanced multi-agent reinforcement learning."** https://scholar.google.com/scholar?cluster=16064055166298116199
  State of the art on mine intersection delay: intersections are modeled as explicit conflict zones with scheduling, confirming junction delay is a first-class term omitted by link-only models.
- **Biniaris, Xiroudakis, Saratsis et al., 2025, *Circular Economy and Sustainability* (Springer), "Minimization of CO2 Emissions in Openpit Mines by Using Stochastic Simulations."** https://scholar.google.com/scholar?cluster=15822385433262914986
  Represents route congestion and waiting with **M/M/1 queueing** — an example of the queue-based (not VDF-based) treatment common in mining.
- **Setiawan, Haswika, Qurtubi et al., 2025, *Jurnal Sistem dan Manajemen Industri* (Indonesia), "Discrete-event simulation of truck–excavator systems in surface mining using a finite-source closed-loop queuing model."** https://scholar.google.com/scholar?cluster=7414646431005349607
  Indonesian surface-mining context: finite-source closed-loop queueing + DES, the standard toolkit; again no BPR.
- **Koryagin & Voronov, 2017, *Transport Problems*, "Improving the organization of the shovel-truck systems in open-pit coal mines."** https://scholar.google.com/scholar?cluster=5210775609760719081 — dispatch policy as the lever to reduce truck bunching on routes.

**Typical findings across this literature:** (i) bunching behind the slowest vehicle is the dominant congestion mechanism on no-overtaking mine roads and scales with fleet speed heterogeneity; (ii) losses from road interaction are additional to, and often comparable with, endpoint queuing; (iii) congestion at intersections and passing constraints is modeled by explicit conflict/scheduling logic; (iv) nobody calibrates a BPR curve for a mine road in the peer-reviewed mining literature we could find — the closest analogue is queueing-theoretic delay.

## 2. BPR critique: validity at v/c > 1 and alternatives

**Finding: the transport literature is unambiguous that static BPR is a steady-state device whose oversaturated branch is a mathematical extrapolation, not physics. For sustained v/c > 1 the recommended tools are (a) Akçelik/Davidson time-dependent functions derived by coordinate transformation from deterministic queueing, or (b) explicit queue-based / kinematic-wave link models (point queue, CTM, LTM).**

- **Akçelik, R., 1991, *Australian Road Research* 21(1), "Travel time functions for transport planning purposes: Davidson's function, its time dependent form and an alternative travel time function."** https://trid.trb.org/view/351740
  The foundational fix. Davidson/BPR-type steady-state curves are invalid "for flows near and above capacity" because steady state does not exist there; Akçelik derives a **time-dependent** function via the coordinate-transformation technique so that beyond capacity delay grows (approximately linearly) with both the demand-to-capacity ratio and the **duration of the oversaturated period** — exactly the regime of your v/c = 3–5 plans. Cited 369+.
- **Spiess, H., 1990, *Transportation Science* 24(2), "Conical volume-delay functions."** https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/trsc.24.2.153
  Proposes the conical VDF and the well-known list of desiderata for VDFs (monotonic, bounded gradient, well-behaved in assignment). Conical fixes BPR's flatness at low v/c and its explosive gradient at high powers, but it is still a static curve — it does not model queue accumulation.
- **Hadi, Shabanian, Ozen, Xiao et al., 2013, Florida DOT / ROSAP report, "Application of dynamic traffic assignment to advanced managed lane modeling"** (and Shabanian, 2014, FIU dissertation, https://digitalcommons.fiu.edu/record/9750). https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/26857
  Direct recommendation on record: **"use of the BPR curve for v/c < 1 and Akçelik formula for v/c > 1, since this formula accounts for [oversaturation delay]."** This is the cleanest citable rule for your case.
- **Pan, Zheng, Guo & Chen, 2023, *Journal of Transportation Engineering, Part A* (ASCE), "Modified volume-delay function based on traffic fundamental diagram: a practical calibration framework for estimating congested and uncongested conditions."** https://scholar.google.com/scholar?cluster=13491157958761244463
  Shows that at v/c = 1 the BPR and conical functions give t/t0 ≈ 2 regardless of what actually happens, and that when "the V/C ratio is greater than 1 the traffic flow is in a [queued regime]" that static VDFs misrepresent; proposes deriving the VDF's congested branch from the fundamental diagram instead. Also flags the classic identification problem: in BPR, v is *demand*, not observed flow — observed flow can never exceed capacity, so calibrating or evaluating BPR at v/c > 1 with counted volumes is ill-posed.
- **Pan, Guo & Chen, 2022, *Transportation Research Record*, "Calibration of dynamic volume-delay functions: a rolling horizon-based parsimonious modeling perspective."** https://scholar.google.com/scholar?cluster=12633722450339596309 — dynamic VDFs needed to capture real bottleneck behavior "in oversaturated conditions."
- **Pan, Hu, List & Zhou, 2026, *Communications in Transportation Research*, "A review of volume-delay functions integrating traditional models and emerging AI technologies."** https://scholar.google.com/scholar?cluster=3421944927105700179 — historical review: the 1980s–90s integration of **queueing theory** into VDFs (Davidson→Akçelik) was precisely a response to BPR's failure near/over capacity.
- **Small, K.A. & Chu, X., 2003, *Journal of Transport Economics and Policy* 37(3), "Hypercongestion."** https://scholar.google.com/scholar?cluster=1584133396482140989
  Economics-side classic: the backward-bending (hypercongested) branch of the speed–flow curve is not a valid supply curve; for severely congested conditions they replace it with a **piecewise-linear average cost function based on simple queueing theory**. Independent confirmation that "price congestion above capacity with a queue, not a curve."
- **Dowling & Skabardonis, 1997 (Transportation Research Record 1591), discussed in Moses, Mtoi, Ruegg & McBean, 2013, FDOT report "Development of speed models for improving travel forecasting and highway performance evaluation."** https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/40540
  Documents BPR's known drawbacks (insensitive below capacity, unrealistic above) that led agencies to updated BPR forms and alternatives (Akçelik, conical).
- **Neuhold & Fellendorf, 2014, *Transportation Research Record*, "Volume delay functions based on stochastic capacity."** https://scholar.google.com/scholar?cluster=18338363130489049316 — capacity is stochastic, a point estimate of c (like a single headway-derived value) biases VDF delay.
- **Queue/kinematic-wave link models for oversaturation.** Szeto & Lo, 2006, *Transportmetrica*, "Dynamic traffic assignment: properties and extensions" (https://scholar.google.com/scholar?cluster=6964761059389259317) and Bliemer, Brederode, Wismans et al., 2012, TRB, "Quasi-dynamic network loading: adding queuing and spillback to static traffic assignment" (https://scholar.google.com/scholar?cluster=14734780450109335637) compare point-queue, cell transmission model (CTM, Daganzo 1994) and link transmission model (LTM) loading: when demand exceeds capacity, delay comes from a growing physical queue with spillback — behavior a static curve cannot produce. Frederix, Tampère & Viti, 2010 (https://scholar.google.com/scholar?cluster=14179085687581411095) show model choice materially changes travel-time estimates in congestion.

**Bottom line for task 2:** BPR with any exponent is indefensible as a *physical* travel-time predictor at v/c = 3–5. Removing BPR2 because it "exploded" treated a symptom: the correct diagnosis is that at v/c > 1 travel time is governed by queue accumulation over the analysis period, so the delay should be `t = t_free + f(queue)`, either via Akçelik's time-dependent form (drop-in replacement, needs a period length T) or an explicit point-queue/CTM link model (structurally right for a corridor with storage and spillback).

## 3. Speed–density on unpaved/haul roads

**Finding: there is no published, calibrated Greenshields-style fundamental diagram for mining haul trucks that we could locate. Mine-truck speed models are dominated by vehicle-dynamics (rimpull–grade–rolling resistance) relationships, not traffic-density relationships. Your RR-based free speed + a congestion overlay is therefore consistent with practice, but the congestion overlay itself has no mining-side empirical FD to lean on.**

- **Coffey, Nikraz & Leek, 2018, *Australian Journal of Civil Engineering*, "Haul road rolling resistance and pavement condition."** https://scholar.google.com/scholar?cluster=11399641846349706109 — regression equations linking rolling resistance to pavement condition and speed; the standard basis for v(RR)-type curves.
- **Tapia, Salazar, Collao, Escudero et al., 2022, *Indian Journal of Science and Technology* (indianjournals.com), "Analysis of the Impact of Rolling Resistance on Performance Indicators of Loading and Hauling Operations in Open-pit Mining."** https://scholar.google.com/scholar?cluster=14522291674212972867 — Chilean data; RR → speed → productivity/fuel KPIs. (This is the same Chilean school as Meneses & Sepúlveda 2023; we could not find the exact Meneses & Sepúlveda paper indexed in Scholar under that name, which suggests it is in a regional/SciELO-type venue with thin indexing — worth pinning down the canonical reference and its fit range before relying on extrapolation.)
- **Orkhontuul, Byambadagva et al., 2025, *Mongolian Journal of ...* (mongoliajol.info), "Research on determination of haul truck speed depending on mining and technical conditions."** https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Orkhontuul+haul+truck+speed — speed set by road class/grade/condition, again vehicle-dynamics not density.
- **Soofastaei, 2016, UQ thesis (above)** — effects of payload, truck speed and total resistance; bunching treated by simulation, not by an FD.
- **Kucharski & Drabicki, 2017, *Journal of Advanced Transportation*, "Estimating macroscopic volume delay functions with the traffic density derived from measured speeds and flows."** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2017/4629792 — methodological template if you ever want to *fit* a VDF/FD from your own GPS/dispatch data (works from measured speeds and flows, handles the congested branch by density binning). This is directly transferable to WBN telemetry.
- **Sharma, Swami & Swami, 2012, *International Journal for Traffic and Transport Engineering*, "Speed-flow analysis for interrupted oversaturated traffic flow with heterogeneous structure for urban roads."** https://scholar.google.com/scholar?cluster=13973327254094415334 — heterogeneous, oversaturated, interrupted flow needs locally calibrated curves; measured results "resemble updated BPR" only after recalibration.

**Greenshields vs measured:** for highway trucks the linear Greenshields FD is known to fit poorly at high density; for mine roads nobody has published a validated FD at all. The de facto mine-road "fundamental diagram" is implicit: free speed from rimpull/RR, capacity from a fixed following headway, and a vertical queue when demand exceeds it — i.e., a triangular/trapezoidal FD assumption. Making that assumption explicit (Newell's triangular FD, which underlies LTM/CTM) would put your model on standard theory with only two extra parameters (jam spacing, wave speed).

## 4. Capacity basis used in mining studies

**Finding: three bases appear, in this order of prevalence: (1) simulation-derived throughput (DES with no-overtaking and spacing rules — capacity is an *output*), (2) queueing-theoretic service rates at shovels/dumps/crushers, (3) headway/spacing-based road capacity like yours — used mainly in design guidelines and safety rules rather than as a calibrated flow capacity.**

- **Tannant & Regensburg, 2001, *Guidelines for Mine Haul Road Design* (UBC).** https://open.library.ubc.ca/soa/cIRcle/collections/facultyresearchandpublications/52383/items/1.0102562 — the standard design reference; following-distance rules (fixed-distance rules of thumb such as your 50 m, or time-based 2–4 s spacing scaled with speed) are **safety minima**, not measured saturation headways. A capacity computed from them is a ceiling on safe flow, not an observed max flow.
- **Zeng, Baafi & Walker, 2019 (above)** — capacity emerges from simulated spacing + no-overtaking + speed distributions; they do not impose a trucks/hour figure.
- **Biniaris et al., 2025 and Setiawan et al., 2025 (above)** — queueing capacity (M/M/1, finite-source closed network) at service points; road links are travel-time legs.
- **Miao & Zhao, 2024; Gamache et al., 2023 (above)** — single-lane capacity governed by passing-bay scheduling (meet/pass conflicts), i.e., capacity is direction-interaction-dependent, which your one-lane-per-direction assumption hides at bays/junctions.
- **Neuhold & Fellendorf, 2014 (above)** — highway-side evidence that capacity should be treated as stochastic (weather, surface condition, fleet mix), which matters doubly on unpaved roads where RR and hence speed bins shift daily.
- **Hazrathosseini, 2026, Université Laval thesis (corpus.ulaval.ca), "Use of artificial intelligence for transportation systems (dump trucks) in surface mines..."** https://scholar.google.com/scholar?cluster=16966328949923157 — recent AI/RL line: congestion managed by learned dispatch policies; again capacity emergent.

**Assessment of your basis:** headway-based c = v_slowest-bin / 50 m is defensible as a *planning* capacity and has the virtue of transparency. Two caveats from the literature: (i) a fixed 50 m distance implies capacity rises linearly with speed, whereas time-headway-based spacing (2–4 s + truck length) gives a flatter, more realistic capacity curve, and empirically saturation headways on constrained roads exceed safety minima (drivers do not pack at the legal minimum); (ii) using the slowest speed *bin* conflates the bunching mechanism (slow leader) with capacity — in a no-overtaking corridor the sustained capacity past any point is set by the slow vehicle's *own* headway stream (a moving bottleneck), so flow constraint and platoon speed should be modeled together, not folded into one c.

## Verdict

**1. Is the approach defensible?** Partially. Using BPR with α=0.15, β=4 in the *undersaturated* regime (v/c ≲ 0.9) is standard and fine for a planning-grade simulator. Headway-based capacity is a reasonable, transparent planning basis and is at least as principled as anything published for mine roads. The RR→speed regression matches how the mining literature sets free speeds. **But applying any static BPR at v/c = 3–5 is outside the function's validity domain by consensus of the transport literature** (Akçelik 1991; Small & Chu 2003; Pan et al. 2023; Hadi et al. 2013). At those ratios your reported congested travel time is an artifact of an extrapolated polynomial: with plain BPR at v/c=3 the model says t = 13.2×t_free instantly and statically, when the true physics is a queue that grows over the shift — delay depends on *how long* demand exceeds capacity, which BPR cannot express. Removing BPR2 didn't fix this, it just chose a gentler wrong curve.

**2. Single highest-value improvement:** replace the BPR tail with a **capacity-constrained queue formulation**. Concretely, the cheapest robust option that stays within your current architecture is **Akçelik's (1991) time-dependent function**: t = t_free + 0.25·T·[(x−1) + √((x−1)² + 8·J·x/(c·T))], x = v/c, T = analysis period (e.g., 1 h), J = delay parameter — it reproduces BPR-like behavior below capacity and goes *linear* in x above capacity with the physically correct dependence on period length, and it is the form explicitly recommended for v/c > 1 (Hadi et al. 2013). The structurally better option, if you can afford one more step, is a **point-queue or link-transmission (Newell triangular FD) link model** per section, which also gives you queue location/spillback along the 68 km corridor and a natural home for passing-bay and junction bottlenecks (Szeto & Lo 2006; Bliemer et al. 2012).

**3. Second-priority improvement** (and first by mining-literature emphasis): explicit **bunching/moving-bottleneck logic** — in a no-overtaking corridor the platoon speed is the slowest active truck's speed, and section throughput is min(headway capacity, slow-truck passage rate). Zeng et al. 2019 and Krzyzanowska 2007 both show this, not v/c on the loaded lane, is the dominant real-world congestion mechanism on shared mine roads with heterogeneous fleets. A simple implementation: sample the slowest truck speed per section per period from the contractor speed distributions and set section pace = min(BPR/Akçelik speed, slowest-truck speed).

**4. Calibration path:** you have GPS/dispatch data; fit your own speed–flow/density relation per section with the Kucharski & Drabicki (2017) procedure and check the 50 m headway capacity against observed max sustained trucks/hour. No published mine-road fundamental diagram exists to borrow, so your own data is the only credible calibration source.
