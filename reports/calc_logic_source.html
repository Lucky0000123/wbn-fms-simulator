The one rule behind everything

A truck can only be in one place at a time. Its day is 24 hours; every minute spent driving, queuing or being loaded comes out of that budget. The model counts those minutes honestly, then:

trips per truck per day = day minutes ÷ minutes per trip

Where the numbers come from

  • Distances — surveyed chainage (same table as the map)
  • Speeds — official speed-limit sheets + GPS measurements
  • Load / dump times — calibrated per route from dispatch data
  • Payloads — measured tonnes per trip per route
  • Traffic shape — 34 days of GPS, hour by hour, per road

3 · QUEUES AT THE LOADER

More trucks ≠ more tonnes, once the loader is busy

The fleet is a closed loop: N trucks circulate between the road and a few loading faces. We use the standard closed-fleet queue model (machine-repair / M/M/c//N) — the same one used across the mining literature.

How it works

  • Each loader serves one truck every ~5 minutes.
  • If trucks return faster than the loaders can serve, a queue forms — and queue minutes are added to every trip.
  • With too many trucks, output flattens at the loaders' maximum: adding trucks then adds only waiting, never tonnes.
Match check: every plan row also respects trips/hr ≤ loaders × (60 ÷ load minutes) — output can never exceed what the loading faces can physically serve.

Trips per truck vs fleet size (one route)

Left: few trucks — everyone loads immediately. Dark bars: the queue grows; each truck completes fewer trips. The knee is where adding a truck stops paying for itself, and the app marks it on every saturation chart.

4 · ROAD PENALTIES

The road has a capacity, and traffic comes in waves

One loaded lane per direction, 50 m following distance. Capacity = the slowest section speed ÷ 50 m — about 400–600 trucks/hour depending on the section.

Below capacity → small penalty, above → queue

Up to capacity we use the standard road-engineering curve (BPR): delay grows gently, then sharply as v/c approaches 1. Past capacity we switch to a queue formula (Akçelik): delay then grows with how long the overload lasts — a polynomial can't say that honestly.

Traffic is waves, not a flat line

From 34 days of GPS: each road has its own rhythm — KR peaks at the 07:00/19:00 shift changes, the coast road in the evening. Hours range from 0.13× to 2.45× the daily average. Since congestion cost rises steeply with load, pricing at the flat average understates the real day. We price each hour at its measured level.

The standing rules (always enforced)

  • SAP routing: each pit sends ~2,000 t/day to its POS buffer; the rest goes direct to FeNi per the mine plan.
  • BLB accepts RIM trucks only.
  • POS is transit: whatever enters must leave on IWIP reclaim trucks (input = output), sized by the same trip model.
  • Targets = the sales table (SAP 5.72 Mt · LIM-TOS 4.64 Mt · LIM-LD 6.64 Mt for Sep–Dec).
  • Other tenants (MHM, POSITION, PMA…) take road space, never our tonnage — they make our trips slower, not fewer.

Every row also gets…

  • Weighbridges: trips split across the eligible bridges (owner matrix), balancing load — the sheet shows each bridge's share, its utilisation %, and its expected waiting time.
  • Loaders: from the measured trucks-per-loader ratio.
  • Road check: the whole plan's trucks (plus tenants) are put on the corridor hour by hour, and every section gets a GREEN / YELLOW / RED verdict per hour.
Result: tonnes per route per month, fleet, trips, congestion — all from one pipeline, so Excel, the app and the API can never disagree.

A 2026 literature review (115 papers) confirmed: our architecture (physics + closed-fleet queue + road pricing) is the approach the mining research itself recommends for planning tools.

80+ automated gates on every change

  • Fleet is conserved — the plan can never invent or lose trucks
  • Excel = app, cell by cell, for all saved scenarios
  • Congestion advisory never changes tonnage figures
  • Contractor walls (BLB=RIM), POS input=output, sales targets
  • Model curves frozen and diffed — silent drift is impossible
Principle: every number the app shows states its own basis — measured, calibrated, or rule — so a wrong assumption can be found and corrected, not hidden.
